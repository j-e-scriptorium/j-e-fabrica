Twenty Voyages

An atlas of ten voyages that happened and ten that did not, charted on the same coastlines.

By Tom G. Turner.

Ten of these voyages are historical: Columbus's first crossing, Magellan and Elcano's circumnavigation, Vasco da Gama's route to India, Cook's Endeavour, Darwin's Beagle, the Mayflower, the Titanic, Zheng He's Ming treasure fleets, Shackleton's open-boat crossing of the Southern Ocean in the James Caird, and Bligh's twenty-three-foot launch after the mutiny on the Bounty. The other ten never happened: Odysseus's ten years home from Troy, Aeneas out of a burning city, the Pequod's pursuit of Moby-Dick, Sindbad's seven voyages, Gulliver's four, the Argo's run for the Golden Fleece, Coleridge's Ancient Mariner, the Nautilus under twenty thousand leagues, Huck and Jim's raft down the Mississippi, and Marlow's passage up the Congo in Heart of Darkness. All twenty are plotted on the same map, at the same scale, so a reader can see how a route invented in the eighth century BC compares to one sailed in the sixteenth century AD.

Click a name to trace its voyage stage by stage; the map frames itself to the route and a panel names each landfall. Click a second name to compare two at once — the view switches to a latitude profile and a table, so real and invented itineraries can be measured against each other directly rather than just eyeballed. Left and right arrow keys step through a single voyage's stages once one is loaded; drag to pan the map, scroll or use the on-map buttons to zoom.

Every position on the map is marked for how firmly it is known. A filled square is a place and moment stated outright in the source or documented in the historical record. A half-tone disc is inferred — not stated, but placed from a fixed point nearby. A hollow ring is notional: somebody's proposal for a contested location, offered without much confidence. Solid tracks are voyages that happened; dashed tracks are invented ones. A few finer distinctions apply to the fiction: a faint dotted line marks a prologue that precedes the voyage proper (the outward passage of the Bounty, the drift before the Endurance was crushed), and a light dashed line marks movement that is narrated within the story rather than depicted as happening — Odysseus telling the Phaeacians his own history, Marlow recounting his journey from a deck on the Thames, Aeneas at Dido's table.

The coastlines themselves are drawn from Natural Earth's public-domain 1:50m land data, with a handful of rivers hand-traced for the voyages that go up them, and ocean and sea names set as labels rather than pulled from a live map service — the atlas runs entirely from three files, with nothing fetched from outside.
