Thirty-Two Journeys

An atlas of sixteen journeys that happened and sixteen that did not — sixteen by sea and sixteen over land, a river counting as land — charted on the same coastlines.

By Tom G. Turner.

Sixteen of these journeys are historical. By sea: Columbus's first crossing, Magellan and Elcano's circumnavigation, Vasco da Gama's route to India, Cook's Endeavour, Darwin's Beagle, the Mayflower, Zheng He's Ming treasure fleets, and Shackleton's open-boat crossing of the Southern Ocean in the James Caird. By land: Xenophon's Ten Thousand fighting their way out of Persia, Alexander's campaign to the Hyphasis and back through the Gedrosian desert, Xuanzang's pilgrimage to Nalanda and home round the Taklamakan by two different rims, Marco Polo's road to Kublai Khan's summer court, Mansa Musa's gold-strewn hajj across the Sahara, Lewis and Clark's Corps of Discovery, the Chinese Communists' Long March, and Burke and Wills's crossing of Australia and failure to return from it.

The other sixteen never happened. By sea: Odysseus's ten years home from Troy, Aeneas out of a burning city, the Pequod's pursuit of Moby-Dick, Sindbad's seven voyages, Gulliver's four, the Argo's run for the Golden Fleece, Coleridge's Ancient Mariner, and the Nautilus under twenty thousand leagues. By land: Huck and Jim's raft down the Mississippi, Marlow's passage up the Congo in Heart of Darkness, the cattle drive of Lonesome Dove, the scalp-hunters' circuit of Blood Meridian, the two road trips of Lolita, Kim's road and espionage along the Grand Trunk Road, Severian's journey north in The Book of the New Sun, and Frodo's road from the Shire to Mordor in The Lord of the Rings. The last two are invented worlds mapped onto real coordinates their texts don't use directly — the Book of the New Sun's Urth onto the Río de la Plata basin and the Andes, and Middle-earth onto the latitudes Tolkien himself gave it in a 1967 letter — and the atlas says so where they're plotted.

All thirty-two are drawn on the same map, at the same scale, so a reader can see how a route invented in the eighth century BC compares to one marched in the twentieth AD, or a sea crossing to a desert one.

Click a name to trace its journey stage by stage; the map frames itself to the route and a panel names each landfall or waypoint. Click a second name to compare two at once — the view switches to a latitude profile and a table, so real and invented itineraries can be measured against each other directly rather than just eyeballed. Left and right arrow keys step through a single journey's stages once one is loaded; drag to pan the map, scroll or use the on-map buttons to zoom.

Every position on the map is marked for how firmly it is known. A filled square is a place and moment stated outright in the source or documented in the historical record. A half-tone disc is inferred — not stated, but placed from a fixed point nearby. A hollow ring is notional: somebody's proposal for a contested location, offered without much confidence. Solid tracks are journeys that happened; dashed tracks are invented ones. A few finer distinctions apply to the fiction: a faint dotted line marks a prologue that precedes the journey proper (the outward passage of the Bounty, the drift before the Endurance was crushed), and a light dashed line marks movement that is narrated within the story rather than depicted as happening — Odysseus telling the Phaeacians his own history, Marlow recounting his journey from a deck on the Thames, Aeneas at Dido's table.

Sea routes are worked out to go round headlands rather than through them; land routes are drawn more plainly, since crossing land is the point. Coastlines, rivers and lakes are all Natural Earth's public-domain 1:50m data — the world's major rivers and lakes pulled from the same source and at the same scale as the coast itself, rather than sketched in by hand at a cruder resolution — with ocean and sea names set as labels rather than pulled from a live map service. The atlas runs entirely from local files, with nothing fetched from outside at runtime; the map data was fetched once, during the atlas's own construction, from the project's public GitHub mirror. The same coastline, river, and lake data draws the map in Strata.
