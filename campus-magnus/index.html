About the data

Two layers, kept apart

Evidence only draws nothing but documented records: a count, a place, or both, each tied to a named source and year. A record appears in the year it was made and then fades. Filled dots are counts for one division. Rings are whole-tribe totals, and dashed rings are later scholars’ reconstructions. A cross marks a place with no count. The soft halo shows how far off our placement of the described place could be.

How the best guess is built

Population. Each division’s numbers come from a fitted demographic curve rather than from joining dots. The fit uses documented counts, weighted by reliability: about ±10% for reservation-era censuses, ±30% for earlier estimates, ±35% for Clark’s 1805 table and ±45% for later reconstructions. It also uses our own anchor guesses, at ±30%. Whole-tribe totals constrain the sum of their divisions.

Between anchors the population grows smoothly, by default at about half a percent a year. On top of that the model applies dated mortality from the epidemic table below. We set the smoothing so that a typical documented count sits within its stated error (median standardized miss 0.6). The shaded band in each division’s chart is the fit’s one-standard-deviation range, widened by a quarter after the hold-out test described under Validation.

Place. Each track passes through every place a source states, at the date and season the source gives, and through our keyframes between them. There are 742 dated places for the divisions, 117 of them before 1800. They run from the Mdewakanton at Mille Lacs and Ulibarrí at El Cuartelejo in 1706, through Lewis and Clark’s councils, Maximilian’s camps of 1833 and Warren’s ranges of the 1850s, to the battle and treaty grounds of the 1860s and 1870s and the agencies placed year by year in the Indian Office’s annual reports. Where a place and a keyframe fall within a year of each other, the documented place wins. For the nomadic peoples the track is a smooth curve through these points that never overshoots them. A village people’s track holds at each place and moves to the next at the documented date of the relocation, or over two years around the midpoint where no date is known, because villages moved by relocating, not by drifting. A place stated only as a range, such as Warren’s Hunkpapa who “roam from the Big Shyenne up to the Yellowstone,” anchors the track at the range’s centre and keeps at least the range’s radius as its uncertainty. A camp that held only part of a division, such as Crazy Horse’s following in 1876–77 while most Oglala were at the agency, stays off that division’s track; so does a trading visit, such as the Crow tipis at the Mandan villages in 1825 and 1833. The uncertainty halo is fitted to how well the track predicts documented places it was not given (see the list of modelling choices).

Seasonal round. This is optional. Each nomadic track runs through the centre of the division’s yearly round. With the seasonal round on, the division moves between a winter ground and a summer ground on either side of that centre. It stays on the winter ground until mid-March, reaches the summer ground by early July, leaves it in early August and is back on a winter ground by late October. The distance and direction between the two grounds change slowly over the decades. They are fitted to the division’s own places dated to a season, such as a sun dance, a winter camp or a battle with a month, from within about five years. Where there are none, they come from the literature on each people’s round. Ray and Moodie have the Cree and Assiniboine wintering in the parkland and summering on the grassland. Ewers has the Blackfoot wintering in the river valleys under the mountains, and Hämäläinen the Comanche in sheltered valleys. West puts the Cheyenne and Arapaho at the Big Timbers of the Arkansas in winter, and Tabeau the Yanktonai at their summer fair on the James. Where neither source exists, the division has no seasonal move of its own and only its bands’ scatter moves with the seasons. Every place dated to a season is reproduced exactly at its date.

We withheld each of 264 season-dated places in turn and predicted it from the rest. The prediction misses by a median 120 km, against 127 km with no seasonal move. The gain comes where the record is dense, above all the Kiowa and Plains Apache. The calendar of the move is the least certain part. Withheld places can only just tell a plateau-and-ramp calendar from a smooth wave peaking in late July (120 km against 122). Among calendars whose misses lie within 3 km of each other, the one used is the one closest to the ethnographic year of winter camps, spring hunts, summer gathering and autumn hunt. Where there are records to test the literature patterns against, they neither help nor hurt. For the divisions that rely on them, the direction of the seasonal move is sourced but untested. With the seasonal round on, the uncertainty halo also carries the uncertainty of the move, widened so that it holds 68% of the withheld season-dated places.

Reading the seasonal round. With the seasonal round on, buttons under the timeline set the month. The up and down arrow keys step one month, and the left and right keys step one year at the same month. Select a nomadic division to draw its round: a line from its winter ground (filled dot) to its summer ground (open dot), along which the division travels during the year. “Trace each division’s seasonal round” draws them all at once, dashed. Divisions with no round of their own draw no line. Playback slows to a quarter-year a second, so a year takes four seconds.

In camp view, nomadic divisions break into camps of about 25 lodges in winter. Winter camps of that size are the tiyospaye and local-band scale reported across the Plains. In summer they gather into a single circle, as Larocque saw with the Crow in 1805: more than 300 tipis moved together through 47 camps in 75 days.

Three levels of detail

Tribes merges each people’s divisions into one dot at their population-weighted centre. Divisions is the unit the population fit and the tracks are built on. Bands & villages splits each division into the units its own ethnographic lists name: Dorsey’s gentes for the Sioux, Grinnell’s bands for the Blackfoot and Cheyenne, Kroeber’s for the Arapaho and Gros Ventre, Lowie’s divisions for the Crow, Mooney’s for the Kiowa, Denig’s for the Assiniboine and Cree, and named villages for the river peoples.

How many bands, and how big. Every list describes one moment. We fix a typical band size from that moment, the division’s fitted population in the list year divided by the number of bands then named. Before the list year, bands appear in list order as the division grows past multiples of that size, so a small early Oglala has three bands and the 1884 Oglala twenty. Where an earlier population was larger than the list-year one, as with the Blackfoot, Mandan or Arikara, unnamed bands or villages fill the difference. After the list year every named band stays, and they shrink or grow together. Offshoots that a source says split from a parent bud out of the parent’s dot. Bands that a source says merged fold back into their absorber.

Documented counts and losses. Where a source counts one band, as Denig does for each Assiniboine band in 1854 or Long for Red Wing’s village in 1824, that band’s share is pulled toward the count. The pull fades over about twenty years on either side. Attacks that fell on a single camp, such as Sand Creek or Wounded Knee, and the 1849 cholera that Grinnell says nearly destroyed three Cheyenne bands, cut that band’s share from that date. Their death tolls are recalled ranges. The camp sizes are ours.

Movement. At the bands level each band wanders, and the specific sites are a seeded random draw: one plausible history, not a record. The scale of the wandering comes from Mooney’s Kiowa calendar, 1833–68, where summer sun-dance grounds moved a median 141 km from one year to the next (26 pairs), winter camps 87 km (13 pairs), and summer grounds lay 158 km from the following winter camp (15 pairs). Part of each year’s shift is shared by the whole division, standing for the herds moving; each band adds about 40 km of its own scatter. With the seasonal round off, bands drift between yearly hunting grounds. With it on, each band winters in its own camp pulled onto the nearest river, hunts outward in spring, joins the division’s summer gathering in July, scatters to an autumn hunt in August and September and settles into a new winter camp by late October, on the same calendar as the division. Where a documented place is near in time, as with the Kiowa after 1833, the shared shift is damped and only the bands’ scatter remains. Roaming shrinks to a quarter over the three years after a division settles at its agency. Divisions and tribes are drawn at their expected positions, without the random draw.

Village peoples’ hunts. With the seasonal round on, the village peoples leave on their tribal hunts and come back to plant and harvest. The hunting grounds and calendars come from sources read for this build, tribe by tribe, as set out in the table under “Every extrapolation choice”. Examples: the Pawnee summer hunt of July 1872 went south across the Platte and the Republican to the Beaver (Grinnell); the Osage hunted from early June to mid-August beyond the Arkansas toward the Great Saline, and again from October to March (Tixier, 1840); the Omaha left after the second hoeing and returned about 1 September (Dorsey, Fletcher and La Flesche). The eastern Dakota scattered “in different directions” on the deer hunt from October to January (Pond). The Mandan and Hidatsa moved a few kilometres into the timber for winter (Maximilian). Where no source was read (the Ponca, Iowa and Wichita), the pattern is borrowed from a neighbour and says so. Hunting-camp records, such as Massacre Canyon, are shown in the evidence layer but kept out of the division tracks, so a village people’s dot stays at its village.

Camps, villages and agencies. The marker says how a people lived at that moment, not only where. A rounded square is a fixed village: the earth-lodge towns of the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, Omaha, Ponca, Oto, Kansa, Iowa and Missouria, the grass-house towns of the Wichita, the mat-and-bark lodges of the Osage, and the bark summer houses of the Mdewakanton. These peoples farmed maize, beans and squash in the river bottoms and went out on the tribal bison hunt in summer and again in fall or winter (the Osage and Kansa also scattered in small camps for the winter deer and fur hunt), so with the seasonal round on, their squares turn into circles while they are out on the tribal hunt and back into squares when they come home to plant or harvest. A circle is a camp of tipis that moves with the herds. A circle with a hollow centre is a people settled at its agency or reserve, from a year after the date the agency opened for them. None of these peoples was pastoral in the usual sense; the nomads herded horses, sometimes in great numbers, but lived on bison, not on their herds. The Cheyenne change from squares to circles about 1790, when they left their earth-lodge villages for the tipi.

Recorded places win. Sixty-six bands and chiefs’ followings carry their own places, and these override the random draw. For 35 the places come from the sources read (Denig’s Cree bands, Henry’s Assiniboine, Mooney’s Kiowa, Dorsey’s and Long’s Red Wing, the Kaw villages, Lac qui Parle, the Wichita towns). For 26 more the dates and places were checked against Wikipedia and other references and are named in each band’s card (Sand Creek, the Washita, the Marias, Ash Hollow, Wounded Knee, the Cheyenne exodus, Sitting Bull, Big Bear, Kaposia, Bde Maka Ska, the Jicarilla Llaneros and Olleros, the Upper and Lower Lipan, the Pembina Saulteurs, the Wahpeton at Little Rapids, Drifting Goose’s people). Five remain recalled and say so. A dark outline marks a chief’s following, a group that existed only for a documented episode.

    What was checked, and against what

    All 129 forts, posts, missions and early towns were checked for dates and position against Wikipedia, North American Forts, FortWiki or the Kansas Historical Society, and about forty were corrected; each one’s hover names its check and any disagreement between references. Fifty-four of the 66 band tracks are sourced or checked (see above). The epidemic fractions marked “assumed” remain our judgment, and so do the band weights and the camp sizes behind the massacre losses.

    Persons per lodge

    Sources disagree widely on how many people a lodge held. Denig used 4.5 to 6. Hayden counted 5 among the Atsina. Mooney put the Kiowa at 6 to 7, with about 2 warriors to a tipi. Later popular accounts say 8 to 10. We use each source’s own ratio and mark every place where we had to assume one.

    What the band lists can and cannot say

    Band names are documented; the number of bands before a list was taken, their sizes, and most of their positions are not. A band named in an 1884 list may have had a different name, or not existed, in 1800. Several matchings are ours and are flagged in the band cards: Mdewakanton chiefs’ villages to Dorsey’s gentes, Hayden’s c. 1856 bands to Dorsey’s, Denig’s Crow chiefs to Lowie’s divisions, and Skinner’s Cree bands to our three Cree divisions. The Omaha, Ponca, Oto, Missouria and Iowa were organized in clans inside one village. Clans were not separate camps, so those peoples stay one dot at every level.

    Thin spots

    Before 1780 there are almost no counts below the tribal level, so the early decades rest on our anchors and the epidemic table. The northern plains before 1790, the Kiowa and Plains Apache before 1830, and the Crow and Shoshone interior before 1830 are the weakest stretches. The Kiowa after 1833 are the best: they have a documented place nearly every season.

Bands follow the same calendar.Winter camps were long and settled, the summer gathering short, and the moves between quick (Grinnell; Ewers; the Kiowa calendar’s winter and sun-dance entries). Dempsey’s Blackfoot year in the Handbook fits it closely: winter camps in wooded river bottoms from late October, a spring scatter onto the prairies, tribal summer camps toward the Cypress Hills and the Medicine Lodge in August.Withheld places barely tell calendars apart: those tried differ by a few kilometres, and a smooth wave misses by 130 km against 125. The choice among them is ethnographic.
    Seasonal uncertaintyThe halo adds the uncertainty of the seasonal move at that moment, and is widened by 7%.A place in July is less certain than the year’s centre when the direction of the summer move rests on few records or on the literature alone.Holds about 68% of withheld season-dated places; the yearly halo alone holds 63%.
    Bands and villages
    Which listOne ethnographic list per division, dated to the year it describes (Dorsey 1878–84, Grinnell c. 1850–70, Denig 1854–56…).Names are documented; mixing lists from different decades would double-count.Other lists would give other names, not other totals.
    Band sizeThe division’s population in the list year divided by the bands then named (median about 210 people).Bands split as they grew (Wissler, Dorsey); a fixed size makes the number of bands follow population.Documented band and camp counts have a median of about 350 people (29 counts), but observers counted large and notable camps, so our units may be finer than the camps people actually lived in.
    When bands appearIn list order as the division grows; offshoots a source names bud from their parent; unnamed units fill out larger earlier populations; after the list year every named band persists.The order of a list usually puts the old bands first, and Dorsey and Grinnell name some offshoots.Changes which band dots exist before the list year, not the division total.
    Documented band countsPull that band’s share toward the count, fading over about 20 years either side.A count is evidence about its band for some years around it, not forever.A longer fade would keep Denig’s 1854 Assiniboine proportions through the 1870s.
    Chiefs’ followingsTemporary units with our weights for documented episodes.A following existed only for its episode.Weights are ours; the dates and places are sourced or checked for most.
    Movement at the bands level
    Year-to-year wanderingShared division shifts with medians of about 141 km (summer) and 87 km (winter) a year, plus 40 km of band scatter; sites drawn from a fixed random seed.The Kiowa calendar is the only long run of dated camps; it sets the scale.Every viewer sees the same history. A different seed would draw different sites at the same scale.
    Near the recordThe shared shift fades to nothing within about a year of a documented place.The record should win.The Kiowa after 1833 keep only band scatter around their recorded camps.
    After the agenciesRoaming shrinks to a quarter over three years after a division settles at its agency.Reservation life confined movement, though hunts and visits continued (the Kiowa calendar keeps moving to 1890).The late dots cluster around agencies.
    Winter campsPulled onto the nearest mapped river within 60 km.Winter camps sought timber, water and shelter in valleys (Grinnell; Maximilian; Pond).Places winter camps on rivers rather than open upland.
    Bison rangeFrom 1850, each hunting division’s centre is checked year by year against the range of the herds. The range is traced from Hornaday’s map of the extermination.A people living on bison could not stay long where there were none.Among the bison-hunting peoples only the Siksika (1879–81) and the Cypress Hills Cree (1879–83) fell outside the range. Both have keyframes that take them into Montana after the last herds and back again, as Dempsey describes. The other division-years outside it belong to peoples no longer living mainly on bison: the Jicarilla, the Lipan, the eastern Dakota and the Turtle Mountain Ojibwe.

    Validation

    The test is strict in one way and lenient in another. It removes a count and its own weight entirely, but it keeps the whole-tribe totals allocated as in the full fit, so a little of the removed count can leak back through its tribe’s total. The largest misses are the source conflicts listed below. The most informative comparison is with plain interpolation between a division’s neighbouring counts. The model does slightly better while also giving a range and a value for every year, not only between counts.
