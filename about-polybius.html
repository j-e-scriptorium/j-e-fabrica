Polybius, book by book

The scroll and the map: every surviving book of the Histories, walked across the Mediterranean in the order Polybius wrote it.

Polybius set out to explain a single fact: how Rome came to rule almost the entire inhabited world in fifty-three years. Doing that meant narrating Italy, Greece, Macedon, Africa, and Asia Minor together rather than one after another, because after 220 BC their histories stop being separable — a war in Spain has consequences in Greece within the same book. Polybius's own word for this is symplokē, "interweaving": the moment the Mediterranean's separate stories become one story. This exhibit takes that seriously and lets you walk a book the way he built it, rather than the way a modern chronology would sort it.

Pick a book — from Book 1's account of Rome's rise through the First Punic War to the fragments of Book 40 — and a rail beneath the map lays out that book from its first episode to its last. Each mark on the rail is one episode, colored for whichever power is acting: Rome, Carthage, a Greek city or league, Macedon, and so on. Playing the rail steps the map through the book in sequence, so the action visibly jumps theater to theater exactly as Polybius narrates it, rather than settling in one place before moving to the next.

The map underneath sizes each place by how often the book returns to it — a rough gauge, book by book, of where Polybius's real center of gravity lies, which is not always where the Histories' famous set-pieces are.
